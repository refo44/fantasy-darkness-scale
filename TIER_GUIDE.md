# Tier Guide

A descriptive guide to what each of the scale's 10 tiers actually contains — the kind of works
that land there, the score patterns typical of the tier, and representative examples from the
current 92-work catalog. This reflects the v2 methodology (`Fantasy_Grimdark_Scale_v2_WIP.xlsx`)
and the accepted relabeling of tiers 1–3 (see below).

## How the scale works, briefly

Seven criteria, each scored 0–4 per work (see `CRITERIA_THEORY.md` for what each one is a
construct of, and the xlsx Methodology sheet for the literal Spanish-language anchor text):
Structural Despair, Limited Heroism, Moral Cynicism, Structural Corruption, Redemption
Difficulty, and Narrative Acceptance of Injustice are each weighted 0.15; Explicit Darkness is
weighted 0.10 (it tracks surface-level depiction, not theme, so it's kept slightly lighter and
can diverge from the other six).

`Weighted Internal Score = Σ(criterion × weight)` → `Final Score = 1.5 + Weighted × 2.25` →
`Tier = FLOOR(Final Score)`. This gives a `1.5–10.5` range and guarantees the displayed score's
leading digit always matches its tier.

## Tier 1 — Very Bright Fantasy `[1.5–2.0)`

*(Relabeled — the tier's previous full name, "Wholesome Cozy Fantasy, Gentle Hopepunk & Very
Bright Fantasy," described three descriptive threads that still characterize this tier even
though "Very Bright Fantasy" is now its official name on its own. Both threads below are still
real, current sub-categories within the tier.)*

The scale's floor. Four of five works here (My Neighbor Totoro, Legends & Lattes, Kiki's
Delivery Service, Ponyo) score a literal all-zero across every criterion — no antagonist worth
the name, no institution to critique, nothing needing redemption, no injustice depicted. Virtue
isn't tested here, it's simply how the world works. Final Fantasy I is the tier's one outlier
(Explicit Darkness 1) — even its minimal, abstract peril (four Light Warriors restoring
corrupted Crystals) barely registers.

- **Wholesome Cozy Fantasy**: the only work on the entire scale carrying the true Cozy Fantasy
  genre tag sits here — Legends & Lattes, genuinely part of that comfort-focused, found-family
  book-publishing genre, not just cozy in feeling.
- **Gentle Hopepunk**: lives in its purest form here — Totoro, Kiki's Delivery Service, and
  Ponyo, where no real adversity is needed at all; kindness and community are simply the
  default, deliberately-valued state of the world.

## Tier 2 — Bright Fantasy `[2.0–3.0)`

*(Relabeled — the tier's previous full name, "Resilient Cozy Fantasy, Fierce Hopepunk & Heroic
Fantasy," described three descriptive threads. One of them no longer has a current example in
this tier, noted honestly below.)*

15 works: Harry Potter and the Philosopher's/Chamber of Secrets, The House in the Cerulean Sea,
Final Fantasy III & V, Slayers, Castle in the Sky, Dungeons & Dragons: Honor Among Thieves,
Legend, The Nightmare Before Christmas, The Little Prince, A Conspiracy of Truths, Willow, The
Chronicles of Narnia, The Legend of Zelda, DanMachi.

A real antagonist and real stakes show up, but heroism resolves things decisively — Limited
Heroism almost never exceeds 1 here. Where institutions appear at all, they carry at most one
flaw (Structural Corruption rarely above 1) rather than systemic rot. Explicit Darkness is often
this tier's *most* elevated axis (FF III & V, Slayers, Legend, and Nightmare Before Christmas
all reach 3) — proof that a work can look and sound genuinely intense (a scary villain, macabre
imagery, frequent on-screen peril) without needing systemic despair to justify it.

- **Resilient Cozy Fantasy**: no current work in this tier is tagged with the Cozy Fantasy genre
  (the only tagged example on the whole scale, Legends & Lattes, sits in Tier 1) — worth noting
  honestly rather than forcing a fit. This descriptor doesn't currently have a live example.
- **Fierce Hopepunk**: first appears here at its lightest — The House in the Cerulean Sea, real
  institutional prejudice resisted through kindness, but still cleanly and completely resolved.
  (A Conspiracy of Truths is also tagged Hopepunk in this tier, though its specific variant
  isn't documented in the scoring record.)
- **Heroic Fantasy**: the tier's dominant shape — a clean quest, a villain who gets defeated, a
  durable happy ending (Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone, Castle in the Sky, D&D: Honor
  Among Thieves, Willow, The Chronicles of Narnia, The Legend of Zelda, and most of the rest of
  the tier).

## Tier 3 — Moderately Bright Fantasy `[3.0–4.0)`

*(Relabeled — the tier's previous full name, "Balanced, Bittersweet Hopepunk or Melancholic
Fantasy," described three descriptive threads. All three are still real and current — this is
the most tonally diverse tier on the scale, which is exactly why it needed a flavor-agnostic
official name rather than a mood word.)*

17 works. Structural Despair becomes common for the first time in this tier (many works score
1–2), as settings begin carrying real historical weight or genuinely existential stakes rather
than a contained, resolvable threat.

- **Balanced**: comedy and darkness genuinely interwoven, neither masking the other — Discworld,
  Dungeon Meshi, Cormyr, Record of Lodoss War, Black Clover.
- **Bittersweet Hopepunk**: real, existential loss (mortality, time) processed through
  connection chosen anyway, knowing it costs grief later — Frieren: Beyond Journey's End
  (the scale's defining example of this shape), Mushishi, Final Fantasy II & IV, Nausicaä,
  Spirited Away.
- **Melancholic Fantasy**: epic, elegiac heroic fantasy where victory carries real melancholy —
  The Lord of the Rings, The Hobbit, Howl's Moving Castle.

A handful of entries (Neverwinter Nights, Baldur's Gate I) don't fit any of those three threads
specifically — they're straightforward heroic CRPG adventure at moderately elevated stakes. That
they still belong here without forcing a mood-fit is the point of a light-intensity official
name instead of a flavor-specific one.

## Tier 4 — Fantasy in Gray Tones `[4.0–5.0)`

*(High stakes, real losses, but still high hope — unchanged label.)*

16 works: The NeverEnding Story, Witch Hat Atelier, Baldur's Gate II: Shadows of Amn, Harry
Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban, Grimgar, Avatar: The Last Airbender, Earthsea, Final Fantasy
XII, The Legend of Vox Machina, Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood, The Boy and the Heron, Final
Fantasy IX, Ranking of Kings, Tales from Earthsea, Adventure Time, Star vs. the Forces of Evil.

The scale's real pivot: a work here is neither simply heroic nor simply dark, but genuinely
both. Structural Corruption commonly reaches 2 (a real, notorious corrupt institution — though
not the whole world) and Redemption Difficulty starts requiring 2 (real sacrifice, not just
effort). Avatar: The Last Airbender anchors this tier's **Fierce Hopepunk** — real genocide
backstory and active institutional oppression, met with organized found-family resistance.

## Tier 5 — Gloomy Fantasy `[5.0–6.0)`

*(Unchanged label.)*

18 works — the largest tier in the catalog: Dragonlance, Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire,
Elantris, Baldur's Gate III, The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim, The Silmarillion & Great Tales, The
Legend of Korra, Princess Mononoke, The Dark Elf / Drizzt, The Wheel of Time, Final Fantasy VI,
The Kingkiller Chronicle, Goblin Slayer, Final Fantasy X, Final Fantasy VII, Harry Potter and
the Order of the Phoenix/Half-Blood Prince, Final Fantasy XV.

Structural Corruption and Explicit Darkness sit consistently at 2–3 — a genuinely corrupt
central institution, or war-epic-scale violence, is the norm here rather than the exception.
Most of these are still "good triumphs" stories, but the cost is real and visible: sustained
war, institutional rot, or a devastating personal loss sits right alongside the eventual win.
The Legend of Korra carries this tier's **Fierce Hopepunk** — organized resistance to real
fascism, heavier and costlier than Avatar's tier-4 version of the same shape.

## Tier 6 — Dark Fantasy `[6.0–7.0)`

*(Unchanged label.)*

15 works: Clevatess, Re:Zero − Starting Life in Another World, The Witcher, Final Fantasy XIV,
Shin Sekai Yori, Made in Abyss, Fullmetal Alchemist (2003), The Saga of Tanya the Evil, Claymore,
Final Fantasy XVI, Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows, Mistborn, Märchen Crown, Dark Souls
I–III, The Stormlight Archive.

Genuinely heavy without tipping into nihilism. Explicit Darkness hits its ceiling (4) for several
works here — extreme content becomes a recurring, central feature rather than an occasional
beat. Structural Despair and Structural Corruption regularly reach 2–3: institutions here are
frequently not just flawed but actively built to sustain exploitation (Mistborn's Final Empire,
the Ministry in Deathly Hallows). **No work at this tier or darker carries the Hopepunk tag** —
past this severity, "kindness as the organizing response to adversity" stops being the right
frame even where genuine found-family warmth exists (Re:Zero and Clevatess both have real
found-family cores, but the surrounding darkness dominates too much page-time for the tag).

## Tier 7 — Extreme Dark Fantasy `[7.0–8.0)`

*(Unchanged label.)*

1 work: Berserk (7.4625). Currently a "solo" tier by content, not just formatting — the only
work on this catalog scoring Structural Despair 3, Moral Cynicism 3, *and* Redemption
Difficulty 3 together with Explicit Darkness at its ceiling. This is where a story's basic moral
physics genuinely inverts: cruelty is regularly rewarded, not merely unpunished.

## Tier 8 — Grimdark `[8.0–9.0)`

*(Unchanged label — the genre's own namesake register.)*

3 works: A Song of Ice and Fire, Elden Ring, Fire Punch. Every criterion sits at 3 or higher for
at least one axis; several works hit 3 across nearly the whole profile. No institution is safe,
virtue is frequently a liability rather than unrewarded, and named-character death is the norm.

## Tier 9 — Extreme Grimdark `[9.0–10.0)`

*(Unchanged label.)*

1 work: The First Law (9.825), profile `[4,4,4,4,3,3,4]` — every axis at or near the absolute
ceiling, except Redemption Difficulty and Narrative Acceptance of Injustice, held at 3 rather
than 4 (redemption is agonizingly rare, not literally impossible). A deliberate genre
subversion: standard epic fantasy's "chosen ones" explicitly do not get the ending that
structure usually promises.

## Tier 10 — Very Extreme Grimdark `[10.0–10.5]`

*(Unchanged label.)*

1 work: Warhammer 40,000 (10.5) — the scale's literal, exact ceiling, every one of the seven
criteria at maximum. Not darkness within an otherwise hopeful universe, but a setting built
around the thesis that hope itself is a category error.

## Cozy Fantasy and Hopepunk: the descriptive tags

Two additional columns in the Evaluations sheet, deliberately **excluded from the score
formula** — genre stance toward darkness is not the same thing as amount of darkness (Avatar:
The Last Airbender is tagged Hopepunk but depicts real war and genocide).

**Cozy Fantasy = Yes** requires genuinely belonging to that specific book-publishing genre
(comfort-focused, low-stakes, community/found-family) — not just "has a cozy feeling." Anime
films like Totoro don't qualify even though they're cozy in tone; Legends & Lattes does.

**Hopepunk = Yes** requires fitting one of three variants. A hopeful tone or happy ending alone
is never sufficient:

- **Gentle** — no real adversity needed; kindness, community, and anti-cynicism are simply the
  default, deliberately-valued state of the world. *Examples: Totoro, Kiki's Delivery Service,
  Ponyo, Legends & Lattes — all Tier 1.*
- **Fierce** — real external/societal adversity (an oppressive system, empire, institution) met
  with hope and kindness as *active resistance* to it. *Examples: The House in the Cerulean Sea
  and A Conspiracy of Truths (Tier 2, lightest form) → Avatar: The Last Airbender (Tier 4) →
  The Legend of Korra (Tier 5, heaviest form) — the same shape escalating in cost across tiers.*
- **Bittersweet** — real existential adversity (mortality, loss, time — not something a
  revolution could fix) met with connection chosen anyway, knowing it costs grief later.
  *Defining example: Frieren: Beyond Journey's End (Tier 3). Also Mushishi (Tier 3) — arguably
  the purest fit on the whole scale, since its entire episodic structure is built around
  "loss is real and often permanent, and connection persists anyway."*

The tag disappears entirely past Tier 5 — not because found-family warmth stops existing in
darker works, but because at that severity the surrounding content dominates enough page-time
that "kindness as the organizing response to adversity" is no longer the most honest
description of the work.
